Failed tank

A failed tank in La Mesa needs a decision today

A tank that has given out leaves you with a wet floor and no hot water, so the 24/7 line is staffed for exactly this call. Water heater replacement in La Mesa, CA means removing the old tank and installing a new one that matches your fuel, your venting and your household demand. Water damage grows the longer a failed tank sits, which makes this different from a planned upgrade.

Our job on an emergency call is to stop the water, confirm the tank is the problem and give you a price before any work starts. Then we plan the swap around what you actually need. If you are building out a new space or moving the unit, a planned installation of a new or relocated heater is covered on its own page.

Replacement day

What happens from the old tank to the first hot shower

  1. Shut off and drain

    We close the water and the gas or power, then drain the old tank through a hose to a safe spot outside or to a floor drain.

  2. Disconnect and remove

    The old connectors, vent and relief valve piping come off, and the dead tank goes out of the house.

  3. Set, pan and strap

    The new unit goes on a drain pan where space allows, and straps or a bracing kit hold it to the wall as California Health and Safety Code section 19211 expects.

  4. Connect and vent

    New water connectors, a shutoff valve, the gas flex and sediment trap or the electrical connection, an expansion tank where needed and a proper relief valve discharge pipe all go in.

  5. Fill, light and test

    The tank is filled and purged of air before power or gas goes on, because a dry electric element burns out in seconds. Then we check for leaks, test draft on gas units and walk you through the temperature setting.

Urgent

Flooded garage or closet from a failed water heater

A split tank can empty dozens of gallons onto a garage slab or a closet floor, and the water soaks into drywall and boxes fast. We take these calls at any hour, and a same-day visit is possible when the schedule allows. These first steps keep the damage down while you wait.

  • Close the cold supply valve above the tank. If it is stuck, close the main water shutoff for the house.
  • Switch off the electric breaker or turn the gas control to off before you go near standing water.
  • Do not wade into water that touches a powered heater or outlet. Stay out until the power is off.
  • Lift cardboard, rugs and stored boxes onto blocks or carry them out of the wet area.
  • Push or wet-vac the water toward a floor drain or the door, then run a fan along wet drywall edges.
  • Photograph the tank, its data plate and the wet areas before you clean up.

Checks before the new tank goes in

Sizing, venting, gas line, expansion tank and bracing

A new heater is only as good as the plumbing around it. These are the items we look at on every swap.

Sizing

The number to match is the first-hour rating, which is how much hot water the tank delivers in an hour. A bigger tank also needs a bigger footprint and sometimes bigger venting, so we weigh both.

Venting

Gas units need a flue that slopes up and draws properly. We test draft after the burner lights, and corroded vent pipe is replaced rather than reused.

Gas line check

The gas flex connector, shutoff and sediment trap are inspected and leak-tested. A kinked or rusted connector comes out.

Expansion tank

A closed plumbing system cannot absorb the extra volume of heated water. An expansion tank protects the new tank and its relief valve from pressure spikes.

Earthquake bracing

Section 19211 calls for water heaters to be braced, anchored or strapped against falling and sideways movement. A pre-engineered strapping kit covers standard units up to 120 gallons.

Pan and discharge

The relief valve needs a discharge pipe that ends in a safe, visible spot. A pan under the tank catches a slow leak before it reaches the floor.

Paperwork and fuel changes

Permits, and what a gas to heat pump switch involves

Permit rules vary, so ask the City of La Mesa building staff before work starts. Swapping a gas tank for a heat pump water heater is a bigger change than a like-for-like replacement. It needs a dedicated electrical circuit, room for airflow, a condensate drain and a spot that tolerates cooler exhaust air. Most homeowners do this as a planned job after the emergency is over, and if you would rather retire the tank entirely, a tankless unit brings its own gas line and venting demands.

What changes the final number on a replacement

No dollar figure is honest until someone has seen the tank, the venting and the closet. You get a clear price before work starts, and these are the factors behind it.

Tank size and type

A 50 gallon gas unit and a heat pump unit differ in equipment and in the work needed around them.

Venting and gas work

Old or undersized flue pipe, a rusted gas connector or a moved gas stub adds labor.

Location and access

An attic, a tight closet or a second-floor landing takes more time to work in than a garage wall.

Code items brought up to date

Straps, a pan, an expansion tank, a shutoff valve and a proper discharge pipe are part of a safe swap.

Timing and cleanup

A same-day or after-hours install and the removal of the old tank are priced into the visit.

Homeowner policies often cover sudden and accidental water damage but often not gradual leaks or worn-out equipment, so ask your insurer. The tank itself is usually your cost, and the flood damage may be a separate question.
