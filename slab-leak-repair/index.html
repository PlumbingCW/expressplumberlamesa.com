The problem

A slab leak is a pressurized pipe leaking under your floor

A slab leak is a leak in a water supply pipe that runs beneath or inside a home's concrete foundation slab. If water is surfacing through your floor in La Mesa, CA, call our 24/7 line, and a licensed plumber will help you through the first steps. Because these pipes hold constant pressure, they keep leaking until someone stops them.

Slab-on-grade homes often route hot and cold lines under the floor, then up into walls at each fixture. The escaping water finds the easiest path through the gravel bed. It may soak the floor right above the pipe, or travel and surface at a door threshold, a crack or the garage edge. That is why the wet spot and the leak are often several feet apart.

Urgent

Water is coming up through the floor tonight

Water rising beneath flooring will not slow down by itself, and a 24/7 plumber is on the line at night and on weekends. Cut the supply first, then protect the floor and the outlets while we sort out the location.

  • Shut the main valve on your house plumbing, usually near the front hose bib or inside the garage.
  • Switch off breakers for outlets and appliances along the wet floor.
  • If the leak looks like a hot line, set the water heater to pilot or turn off its breaker.
  • Lift rugs and pull furniture off the wet area, then run fans if you have them.
  • Do not chip or drill into the floor to look for the pipe, because you could hit another line or a wire.
  • Take dated photos and a meter reading for your insurer.

Symptoms

Five signs that the water is under the slab

A warm spot on the floor

A leaking hot line heats the concrete above it. Bare feet find it before any tool does.

Running water you can hear

With all fixtures off, a faint hiss or rushing sound from the floor means a pressurized pipe is losing water.

Damp carpet or lifting flooring

Baseboards that darken, carpet that stays wet or planks that cup and buckle point to moisture rising from below.

A high water bill with no reason

A meter that keeps turning with every fixture closed is the easiest symptom to confirm. The step-by-step test is on our general leak page.

Weak pressure or slow hot water

Water lost under the floor means less reaches the faucets. Hot water that takes longer to arrive suggests the hot line.

Locating the leak

How the line is pinned down before concrete is touched

  1. Isolate the system

    We close the main, read the meter and test hot and cold sides separately to learn which line is losing water.

  2. Listen along the floor

    A ground microphone picks up the hiss of a leak. The sound grows louder as the sensor nears the pipe, and we mark the peak.

  3. Trace and image

    An electronic pipe locator maps where the line runs under the slab. A thermal camera or moisture meter helps confirm a wet or warm area.

  4. Pressure test the suspect line

    Capping the line and watching a gauge confirms the leak is on that run and not a different one.

  5. Mark it and explain the choices

    You see the marks on the floor and hear the options before any concrete is broken.

Repair options

Three ways to fix a leaking line under the floor

For a single leak, a spot repair is the lowest-disruption fix. If a second leak shows up on the same run, rerouting begins to make more sense. A repeat pattern across the house is when a whole-house repipe deserves a real look.

Garage and foundation

Water at the garage edge or along the foundation

Callers often notice trouble where the garage slab meets the house. Water follows the gap between slabs, so it can appear at a seam far from the pipe. Wet soil outside along the foundation can come from the same under-slab line, a sprinkler leak or roof runoff.

Run the meter test first. If the meter moves with sprinklers off, a supply line is the suspect. If your tank sits in the garage, check the heater connections and the tank itself too, because those leaks are above ground and cheaper to fix than a concrete job.

A long-running leak can wash fine soil from under the slab and leave a void. If you see new cracks in the floor or a door that has begun to stick, mention it when you call so we can plan with that in mind.

Why they fail

What wears out pipes beneath the slab

Copper under concrete can corrode from the outside where it touches the slab or wet soil. It can also rub against rock and gravel as the house moves with temperature swings. Constant pressure on the line speeds up wear at any thin spot.

We open the pipe or inspect a cut sample to see how it failed. Pinholes along a long stretch say the whole run is aging, so a reroute wins. A single failed joint says a spot repair will hold. That finding drives which of the three options we recommend.

What to expect at home: spot repair versus reroute

Spot repair under the slab

  • Dust and noise from breaking a patch of concrete.
  • Floor covering removed above the leak.
  • Water is off to the house for part of the work.
  • Patch is visible in the floor afterward.

Overhead reroute

  • No concrete is broken.
  • Small access holes cut in drywall along the new run.
  • Walls need patching and paint.
  • Old line is capped and left in the ground.
Documentation

Papering the claim before the concrete comes up

If you plan to file with your insurer, tell them before concrete is broken. Ask whether the adjuster wants to see the leak first and whether your policy pays for access costs. Coverage varies a great deal from policy to policy.

Keep dated photos of every damaged area, the meter readings from your test and the written location finding. Keep the removed pipe section if you can. Ask for an itemized invoice that separates the leak repair, the concrete work and the flooring, since insurers often treat those lines differently.

Where the pipe sits
