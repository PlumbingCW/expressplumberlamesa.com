P-Trap Replacement: Fix a Leaking Trap Under Your Sink

If water drips from the U-shaped pipe under your sink, you probably have a loose slip nut or a cracked P-trap. Dry the pipe, tighten the nuts by hand, and watch for drips. If it still leaks, replace the trap. Most replacements take under an hour with a bucket, channel-lock pliers and a new kit.

Quick answer

What a P-trap does and when it needs replacing

A P-trap is the curved pipe under a sink that holds a small plug of water. That water seals the drain against sewer gas, which is why a dry or leaking trap in a La Mesa, CA home can bring a rotten smell along with a wet cabinet. Replacement is a simple job on most sinks.

Replace the trap when it is cracked, corroded through, warped from heat or stripped at the threads. Tightening a slip nut fixes a loose joint. A cracked pipe or a worn washer is not a tightening problem.

Leaking trap or clogged drain

The two problems look alike under a sink, but the fixes are different. Use this to decide before you buy anything.

Leaking trap

  • Drips appear at the nuts or the bottom of the U.
  • Cabinet floor is wet or swollen.
  • Water level in the sink drains normally.
  • Drip increases when the sink empties.
  • Wiping the pipe dry shows the exact spot.

Clog

  • Water drains slowly or backs up in the sink.
  • Gurgling from the drain.
  • Standing water in the basin.
  • Cabinet stays dry.
  • A cable or plunger may help.
The job

Replacing a P-trap, one step at a time

  1. Clear the cabinet and set a bucket

    Empty the cabinet. Put a bucket under the trap, because it holds standing water and whatever debris is inside.

  2. Loosen the slip nuts

    There is a slip nut at each end of the trap. Turn them counterclockwise by hand. Use pliers only on stubborn plastic ones, and do not crush the threads.

  3. Pull the old trap and check the pieces

    Slide the trap off. Look at the tailpiece coming down from the sink and the trap arm going into the wall. If either is cracked or corroded, replace it with the trap.

  4. Test fit the new trap

    Fit the new parts dry, before tightening anything. The tailpiece should drop straight and the trap arm should slope slightly toward the wall. The pieces must line up without strain.

  5. Seat the washers and tighten

    Slide each slip nut on first, then the beveled washer with the bevel facing the joint. Tighten by hand plus a quarter turn with pliers.

  6. Run water and watch

    Fill the basin, pull the stopper and let it drain while you run a dry paper towel over each joint. Check again after an hour.

Common mistake

Why tailpiece alignment matters

Most repeat leaks come from a misaligned joint. If the sink tailpiece sits an inch off from the trap arm, tightening the nut pulls the pipe sideways and the washer never seats flat. Cut the tailpiece shorter or add a trap adapter so the parts meet in a straight line.

The trap arm should also slope slightly toward the wall so water flows out and the U still holds its water seal. Sight along the pipe before you tighten the last nut.

Materials

Plastic or brass, and what else to buy

Plastic (PVC or polypropylene)

Light, cheap and corrosion-proof. It is the usual choice for kitchens and bathrooms and is easy to cut with a hacksaw.

Chrome-plated brass

Looks neater in an exposed vanity and is sturdier. Brass can corrode from the inside if the drain carries harsh cleaners.

Solid brass in heavy gauge

Choose it when the trap is visible or sees heavy use. It lasts for decades but costs more than plastic.

Flexible traps

Corrugated traps trap hair and grease and are prone to leaks. Use rigid pieces when you can.

New washers every time

Even if the trap is fine, replace the slip washers. Old ones flatten and weep.

Bigger picture

When a trap leak points to a larger drain problem

A trap that leaks again after replacement may be under strain from a downstream clog. When the drain backs up, water pressure builds and pushes at every joint. If you fix the trap and the sink still drains slowly, the blockage is further along, and a drain that is slow all the way to the wall needs cleaning rather than new parts.

Another warning sign is a trap that sits low or crooked because the wall stub-out has shifted. That is a repair behind the wall. The same goes for a trap arm that slopes the wrong way or an S-trap, which can siphon dry. Replacing it with a proper P-trap and vent is a small remodel job.

Faucet side

Drips that look like trap leaks

Before you buy parts, make sure the trap is the source. Water can run down a supply line, drip from a faucet base or trickle from a sprayer hose and land in the trap area. Dry everything, then put a dry tissue under each joint in turn.

If the moisture traces up to the fixture, your repair is a leaky faucet or a worn supply connection, not a trap. Fixing the right thing the first time saves a second cabinet cleanup.

Safety

Safety and cleanup

Skip chemical openers

Drain chemicals sit in the trap and splash when you open it. Use gloves and eye protection if you used any.

Dry the cabinet

A wet cabinet floor grows mold in days. Fan it out and replace swollen particleboard.

Watch for sewer smell

If the odor returns after the new trap goes in, the vent or the wall connection may be at fault. Call a plumber.

Know your limits

Anything involving cutting into the wall, cast iron or a glued joint behind drywall deserves a licensed plumber.
