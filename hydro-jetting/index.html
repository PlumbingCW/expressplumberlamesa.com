Symptoms first

Why a drain keeps clogging after it was snaked

Hydro jetting is a cleaning method that sends high-pressure water through a nozzle inside a drain or sewer line to strip off grease, scale and roots. If your line was snaked and clogged again, or is backing up in La Mesa, CA late at night, our 24/7 emergency line at (619) 927-0312 reaches a licensed plumber. Until then, keep water use to a minimum.

Four patterns usually point toward jetting. The clog returns within weeks. A cable stalls at the same distance every time. The line drains slowly but never stops completely. Or a plumber pulled out roots and you want the pipe walls cleaned afterward.

None of these proves jetting is the right move. They tell us the cable is not removing whatever is actually narrowing the pipe, and that is a reason to look closer before spending more.

The method

What hydro jetting does inside the pipe

A jetting machine pumps water through a hose that ends in a nozzle with rear-facing ports. The jets pull the hose forward and scrub the pipe wall in a full circle. The operator sets pressure and flow for the pipe size and material, then works from the clog back toward the access point.

Water does what a cable cannot. It cuts grease off the walls, flushes sand and scale downstream, and shreds fine roots so the pipe returns close to its full inside diameter. Large rooted sections are usually cut with a cable head first and jetted afterward.

Jetting needs a way in. A cleanout is best, because it keeps wastewater out of the house. For one stopped sink or tub, a plain cable clearing at the fixture is the faster and cheaper tool.

Jet it, or look first: when hydro jetting is the wrong call

A jet is only as safe as the pipe it goes into. These lists show where the line gets drawn.

Good candidates for a jet

  • Grease buildup in a kitchen branch or main line.
  • Sand, silt and soap scale lining a sound pipe.
  • Fine roots left behind after a cable has cut the main mass.
  • A line that must be clean before a camera inspection.

Camera first, jet later or not at all

  • Cracked, offset or collapsed pipe, where pressure can widen the damage.
  • Old clay or cast iron that is flaking or thin.
  • A sag that holds water, where a jet only moves debris around.
  • Any line where nobody has seen the inside yet.

Before you agree

What a homeowner should know before saying yes

Ask three questions. What is the pipe made of? Has a camera been run? Will you see the video before and after? A plumber who recommends jetting without those answers is guessing. We would rather start with a camera inspection before any high-pressure work on a line of unknown age.

Expect some mess at the cleanout. A drain that was full of debris releases it all at once when the nozzle breaks through. Wear shoes near the access point and keep pets inside.

Jetting cleans but it does not repair. If a section is cracked or offset, buildup returns right where the pipe is damaged. In that case the money is better spent on a fix than on another visit. For lines past the house wall, see how a main sewer line is cleaned from the cleanout.

The visit

How a jetting visit runs from start to finish

  1. Find the way in

    We locate the cleanout and check its size. If none exists, we pick the next best access, such as a pulled toilet or a roof vent.

  2. Open and check

    A cable or a camera goes first, so we know what is in the line and what the pipe looks like before water pressure is added.

  3. Push through, then scrub

    The nozzle goes forward through the blockage. We then pull back slowly while the rear jets clean the walls.

  4. Look again

    A second camera pass shows whether the walls are clean and whether anything else needs a decision from you.
