On-demand hot water

How a tankless heater works and what it asks of your house

A tankless water heater in La Mesa, CA heats water only when a tap opens, passing it through a heat exchanger instead of storing it in a tank. When flow starts, a sensor fires the burner or the electric elements, and the water leaves hot a few seconds later. There is no standing supply to run out, but there is a flow limit, and that limit shapes every decision about the unit.

The heater is only as good as its supporting systems: a gas line big enough to feed it, a vent path that clears exhaust, clean water through the heat exchanger and a unit sized to your fixtures. This page covers installing, repairing, flushing and sizing, and where homeowners can help.

Many callers tell us they just want an existing unit flushed or serviced, not swapped. Tell the dispatcher that on the call, because scale is behind a lot of poor tankless performance.

Tankless does not mean unlimited. Two showers and a dishwasher at once can exceed a unit's flow, and big households sometimes do better with a larger unit or two. If the job turns out to be larger than you want, a new tank installed on a planned schedule is a fair choice.

Flush and descale

Descaling a tankless heater in very hard water

Helix reported an average hardness of 206 mg/L for 2025, which is 12.0 grains per gallon, with a range of 9.1 to 14.6. On the common USGS scale, that is very hard. Scale forms on a tankless heat exchanger faster than on a tank, because the water is heated hard and quickly, so La Mesa units collect it. Blends vary by street, so test your own tap.

A scaled-up heat exchanger moves heat poorly. The unit cycles more, gives temperature swings, shuts down on overheat or flow codes, and wears out the burner and sensors sooner. The fix is a descaling flush, and the manual for your model gives the interval. Some makers call for yearly service in hard water, so read your manual.

Signs that a flush is due include longer waits for hot water, hot and cold surges, a rumbling noise, a drop in flow and error codes tied to temperature or flow. A unit that has never been flushed is overdue, even if it still runs. Descaling is maintenance, and a flushed heat exchanger often returns to normal.

Vinegar flush or service flush: what is yours to do

A vinegar flush runs white vinegar through the heat exchanger with a small pump and two hoses on the service valves for about an hour, then rinses with clean water. It works only if the unit has isolation valves with flush ports. A service flush adds testing and safety checks that a hose and a bucket cannot.

Reasonable to do yourself

  • Read the manual and note any error codes on the display.
  • Clean the inlet water filter screen.
  • Run a vinegar flush if the isolation valves are installed.
  • Rinse the unit well and restore the valves in the right order.

Leave to a licensed plumber

  • Any work on the gas line, burner or vent.
  • Installing isolation valves on a unit that has none.
  • Error codes that return after a flush.
  • Checking combustion, flow rate and condensate drain.
  • Leaks from the heat exchanger or fittings.
Repair

Tankless faults and error codes that need a closer look

Error codes differ by brand, so read your manual first. These are the groups most calls fall into, and a unit that repeats the same fault after a flush is a job for a proper repair visit.

Ignition failure

The burner will not light or will not stay lit. Causes include a low gas supply, a dirty flame rod or a failing igniter.

Flow or sensor faults

Hot water cuts out when you open a tap partway. A clogged inlet screen, scale or a weak flow sensor is the usual cause.

Exhaust or vent faults

A blocked vent, a bird nest on the termination or a cracked pipe trips a safety shutdown. This is a safety fault, so do not bypass it.

Overheat codes

Heavy scale on the heat exchanger traps heat and trips the high-limit sensor. A flush often clears it.

Condensate trouble

High-efficiency models produce acidic condensate. A blocked drain or a spent neutralizer can stop the unit.

Leaks

Water at the unit may be a loose connection, a failed valve or a cracked heat exchanger. Shut off the water and call.

Sizing

Sizing a tankless heater by flow and temperature rise

Two numbers size a tankless heater. The first is flow, in gallons per minute, added up for the fixtures that run at once. A shower uses roughly 2 gallons a minute and a kitchen faucet a bit more than 1.

The second is temperature rise: the gap between the cold water entering the house and the temperature you want at the tap. Colder incoming water means a bigger rise and less flow from the same unit. We size for your coldest conditions, not an average day, so the shower stays hot in winter.

Gas units

Gas line size and venting for a gas tankless

A gas tankless burner draws far more fuel per hour than a tank heater. The existing gas line to a water heater is often too small, so the pipe is sized by the unit's rated load and by how long the run is. A line that is too small starves the burner and sets off ignition or low-flame codes.

Venting depends on the model. Condensing units vent through plastic pipe with a sloped run and a drain for condensate. Non-condensing units need stainless or specified metal vent pipe. Terminations must stay clear of windows, doors and air intakes, and the manufacturer's installation manual sets the clearances.

Electric units

What an electric tankless needs from your panel

Electric tankless heaters skip the gas line and the vent, but they draw a lot of power. A whole-house model can need several large breakers on 240 volt circuits, and the panel has to carry that load on top of the oven, dryer and air conditioning. Panel capacity, not the heater, is often the deciding factor. Smaller point-of-use electric units suit a single sink or shower without a panel upgrade.

How a tankless install is laid out

  1. Load and flow check

    We add up your fixtures, measure the gas line or panel and choose a unit that fits the real demand.

  2. Fuel and vent prep

    Gas line upsizing, the vent run or the electrical circuits are roughed in before the unit is mounted.

  3. Mount with service valves

    The heater goes on the wall with isolation valves and flush ports on the hot and cold lines, so future flushes do not require cutting pipe.

  4. Commission and test

    We check flow, temperature, combustion on gas units and the condensate drain, then walk you through the display and the maintenance schedule.

Water treatment

A softener or filter reduces the scale load on a tankless exchanger, which stretches the time between flushes. If hard water is the main complaint, a softener or filtration system may be worth pricing alongside the heater.

What decides tankless water heater pricing

Tankless work varies more than tank work, so the real number comes from a look at the gas line, the vent path and the panel. You get a clear price before work starts.

Install or service

A flush, a repair and a full install are different jobs with different labor.

Gas line upsizing

A longer run or a smaller existing pipe adds piping work.

Venting

A condensing unit with a long plastic vent run is different from a short metal vent.

Electrical capacity

A panel with spare room is easier than one that needs an upgrade.

Isolation valves and parts

Adding flush valves, a neutralizer or a replacement sensor changes the scope.

Homeowner policies often cover sudden and accidental water damage but often not wear, scale or planned upgrades, so ask your insurer about a leak claim before you assume.
