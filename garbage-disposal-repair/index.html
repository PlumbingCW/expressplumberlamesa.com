Kitchen sink trouble

Garbage disposal repair and installation in La Mesa, CA

A garbage disposal is a small motor under the sink. It spins a plate with two lugs that push food scraps against a grind ring until they are small enough to flush away. When it jams, leaks or dies, the sink fills and the kitchen is out of action, so our 24/7 emergency line takes disposal calls any hour.

Most disposal failures fall into three groups. A jam locks the motor, a leak lets water out of the housing, or the electrical side fails. The right repair depends on which group you are in, and the symptoms are easy to tell apart once you know where to look.

If your unit is more than about eight to twelve years old and leaks from the body, replacement beats repair. Everything else is worth a diagnosis first.

Try this first

How to free a jammed or humming disposal

  1. Cut the power

    Flip the wall switch off, then unplug the cord or switch the breaker off. Keep your hand out of the drain.

  2. Turn the flywheel

    Most units have a hex socket in the bottom center. Insert the key that came with it and work it back and forth until the plate spins freely.

  3. Pull the object

    Shine a light in the drain. Use tongs to remove a bone, glass shard or utensil you can see.

  4. Press reset

    Wait ten minutes for the motor to cool, push the red button on the bottom, then run cold water and switch it on.

  5. Know when to stop

    If it hums again or trips the reset, the motor may be burned out. Further tries only heat it up.

Where leaks start

Leaks from the back, the bottom or the sink flange

Dry the unit, tape paper towels around it, run water and watch which one gets wet first.

Sink flange

The ring where the disposal meets the sink seals with plumber putty or a gasket. If water seeps at the top, the mounting ring needs resetting.

Back of the unit

The dishwasher inlet and drain pipe connect at the back. A loose clamp is a quick fix, but a cracked fitting calls for a new unit.

Bottom of the unit

A leak here usually means internal seals or the housing failed. Once water reaches the motor bearings, the unit is done.

Drain outlet

The pipe on the side that leads to the trap can weep at the gasket or flange. Replacing the gasket often stops it.

Dishwasher connection

Reconnecting the dishwasher hose after a replacement

A dishwasher drain hose attaches to a small inlet on the side of the disposal. New units have a plastic plug inside that inlet called a knockout. If it is not removed before the hose goes on, the dishwasher cannot drain, and dirty water sits in the tub.

We knock out the plug, fish the loose piece out of the chamber, and clamp the hose with a worm-drive clamp. The hose should rise to a high loop under the counter or connect to an air gap, then drop into the disposal. That prevents drain water from the sink flowing back into the dishwasher.

If you bought a unit and cannot get the hose to line up, a licensed plumber can handle that part of installing new fixtures in one visit.

Buying a new one

Sizing a replacement and choosing the power connection

Disposals are rated by motor horsepower. Match what you cook and how many people use the kitchen.

One-third to one-half horsepower

Fine for light use in a small kitchen. These units jam more often with fibrous scraps.

Three-quarters horsepower

A good middle choice for most families, with enough torque for vegetable scraps.

One horsepower

The strongest residential size, better for big households and frequent cooking.

Hardwired or cord

Most kits include a power cord that plugs into an outlet under the sink. A hardwired unit connects to the circuit directly. If there is no outlet, a licensed electrician or plumber with electrical skill should add one.

What belongs in a disposal and what does not

Treating the unit like a trash can is the number one cause of clogs we find.

Safe in small amounts

  • Soft cooked food scraps.
  • Small citrus peels.
  • Ice cubes for cleaning.
  • Small, soft fruit scraps.

Never put down the drain

  • Grease, fat or oil.
  • Coffee grounds and eggshells.
  • Potato peels, celery, corn husks.
  • Bones, pits or fibrous stalks.
  • Pasta, rice or bread that swells.
When the clog is past the disposal

A disposal that grinds fine but a sink that will not drain

If the disposal spins freely but the sink backs up, the blockage sits in the trap or farther along the kitchen line. Grease cools in the pipe and builds a gummy layer. A plunger rarely clears it, but cabling the kitchen line often does. If the cabinet is wet but the disposal is dry, a leaking kitchen faucet above it is the next suspect.

Safety steps

What to do before anyone reaches into the sink

A jammed disposal looks harmless, but the blades and the motor are not. These steps keep hands safe while you wait.

Cut the power

Turn off the wall switch, then the breaker that feeds the disposal. A switch alone can be bumped back on.

Keep hands out

Use tongs or pliers to lift out a spoon or bottle cap. Never put fingers into the grinding chamber, even when the unit is off.

Skip chemical openers

Caustic drain products sit in the sink and can splash on whoever works on the trap next. Plain hot water is a safer test.

Mop standing water

Water near the disposal's cord or the outlet under the sink is a shock risk. Dry the area before you reset anything.
